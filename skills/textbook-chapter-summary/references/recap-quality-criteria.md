# Chapter summary quality criteria

Run this list over a finished or user-supplied chapter summary. Mark each item met, not met, or "needs verification" when only the author or a subject matter expert can confirm it (for example, currency against the newest exam outline). Fix every item that is not met and that the chapter text lets you fix.

## 1. Content accuracy

- [ ] Every technical statement agrees with the chapter and with current industry standards; nothing contradicts the chapter.
- [ ] Terminology matches the official certification exam vocabulary, with key terms spelled exactly as in the exam objectives.
- [ ] No outdated technology version, deprecated procedure or retired exam topic appears.
- [ ] Any example reflects current real-world practice.
- [ ] Subject matter expert review: always "needs verification" unless the user states it was done.

## 2. Educational quality

- [ ] Every chapter learning objective is addressed by at least one summary point.
- [ ] Every summary point maps to an exam objective or job task; the concepts with the highest exam weight get the most space.
- [ ] Cognitive levels follow the chapter: Remember and Understand for foundations, Apply and Analyze for practical skills and troubleshooting, Evaluate and Create only where the chapter goes there.
- [ ] A self-study learner can use the summary with no instructor: no term or idea depends on context the summary leaves out.
- [ ] The self-assessment prompts let the learner judge their own understanding.

## 3. Language and style

- [ ] Active voice and direct address ("You configure ..."), professional textbook tone, neither casual nor conversational.
- [ ] Average sentence length 12 to 15 words; long sentences split; few modifier clauses.
- [ ] Plain language, about an 8th-grade reading level apart from the technical terms themselves.
- [ ] Ideas restated in new words; at most 1 or 2 direct quotations, used only where the exact wording of a term matters.
- [ ] Precise, measurable wording instead of vague phrases.

## 4. Terms and vocabulary

- [ ] Critical terms (those in the glossary or the exam objectives) are bold and defined in 5 to 10 words at first use.
- [ ] Every acronym is spelled out at first use, for example "DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol)", and used as the acronym afterwards.
- [ ] Related terms are grouped by concept, not alphabetically.
- [ ] Where the field has two accepted terms, or industry and colloquial usage differ, the summary says so briefly.

## 5. Formatting and accessibility

- [ ] Headings nest without skipped levels (H3 or H4 under the chapter heading) and use parallel titles that match every other chapter summary in the book.
- [ ] Numbered lists only for sequences; bulleted lists for parallel items; real list markup, not typed dashes in running text.
- [ ] A comparison table, if any, has 2 to 3 rows and a marked header row.
- [ ] At most one simple diagram, readable in under 30 seconds, with a descriptive caption and alt text.
- [ ] No meaning depends on colour alone; the summary reads correctly in grayscale print and at 200% enlargement.
- [ ] Short paragraphs, each with a clear topic sentence; no wall of text.

## 6. Exam readiness

- [ ] At least one common misconception or confusable pair is corrected, introduced with "Note:" or "Important distinction:".
- [ ] Terms to Know are phrased so each can become a flashcard (term on one side, short definition on the other).
- [ ] The difficulty and wording reflect the exam's question style.
- [ ] The summary suggests where to practise further on the hardest topics.

## 7. Length

- [ ] Word count is in the target range for the chapter's length (150 to 300 words by default; 250 to 350 for chapters over 5,000 words; 100 to 200 for introductory chapters of 2,000 to 3,000 words), roughly 10 to 15 percent of the chapter.
- [ ] Space follows the hierarchy: about 60 percent on primary concepts, 30 percent on supporting concepts and relationships, 10 percent on advanced or optional material.
