# Maturity scoring and roadmap

## Maturity levels (five-level scale)

| Level | Name | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial / ad hoc | Unpredictable, reactive, poorly controlled |
| 2 | Managed / repeatable | Basic process established, planning occurs, requirements managed |
| 3 | Defined | Standardized, documented, integrated across the organization |
| 4 | Quantitatively managed | Measured and analyzed against quantitative objectives |
| 5 | Optimizing | Continuous improvement, innovation, organizational learning |

Three-level scale for a small set or a first pass. The one-line definitions are this skill's reading of the three names against the five-level characteristics; tailor them to the context and state them in Maturity model:

| Level | Name | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial | Unpredictable, reactive, poorly controlled (five-level 1) |
| 2 | Developing | Basic process established, planning occurs, not yet standardized across the scope (five-level 2) |
| 3 | Mature | Standardized and documented across the scope, and measured or improved where evidence shows it (five-level 3 and above) |

Other model families, used when the user names one or the domain calls for it: capability maturity models for process-oriented domains, project management office maturity models, industry or regulatory models, and technology readiness scales for research and development.

## Dimensions

Score each method, or the set as a whole, on these dimensions:

- Process knowledge: how well the method is understood by those who use it.
- Process documentation: quality and accessibility of its documentation.
- Process standardization: consistency across people, teams and locations.
- Process measurement: metrics, indicators and performance tracking.
- Process governance: ownership, accountability, decision rights.
- Process improvement: learning and optimization over time.

Adapt the dimensions to the domain when the user's objectives need it, and say which were changed.

## Evidence per level

Each level is claimed only on observable evidence. Evidence types, in rising order of strength: a policy or stated intent; a written procedure; the procedure in use (records, work products); measurement (metrics collected on a stated frequency); management of the method by its metrics (reviews that change it).

## Strength and coverage

Rate each assessed element on two scales, then assign its level from the pair by a stated rule:

- Strength: how fully the level's characteristics are implemented, as a qualitative judgement or a measure on a scale you state.
- Coverage: the share of the scope (methods, teams or sites) that shows them, in bands 0 to 10%, 11 to 32%, 33 to 65%, 66 to 89%, 90 to 100%.
- Mapping rule: write in Maturity model, before scoring, the rule that turns a strength rating and a coverage band into a level, and apply it to every element. If the user gives no rule, use this skill's default: an element reaches a level when that level's characteristics are largely or fully implemented over at least 66% of the scope (band 66 to 89% or 90 to 100%); its level is the highest one that meets the rule, and level 1 when none does.

## Weighting (composite score)

When a composite score is needed, give each evidence tier a weight and state them. One published example weights policy 15%, procedure 20%, implemented 40%, measured 10%, managed 15%.

## Aggregation

- Method level: from its dimension scores.
- Dimension level: aggregate the criterion or method scores for that dimension.
- Overall maturity: the average or the weighted average across dimensions; state which.
- Report variability: the spread between the highest and lowest dimension, and name any dimension far from the others (an imbalance).

## Gap analysis

For each dimension or method: current level, target level, gap in levels, and the criteria of the next level that are not yet met. Strengths are the dimensions at the higher levels; weaknesses are those at the lower levels.

## Prioritization

Priority score = gap x strategic weight x feasibility, each on a scale you state. Then:

- Quick wins: low effort, high impact; schedule first.
- Critical dependencies: foundational capabilities that other improvements need; schedule before what depends on them.

## Transition requirements (per item)

- Activities that meet the next level's criteria.
- Exit criteria: the evidence that shows the next level is reached.
- Resources, skills and tools needed.
- Effort and timeline estimate.

## Phased roadmap windows

| Phase | Window | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 6 months | Foundations, quick wins, a baseline |
| 2 | 6 to 12 months | Standardization, documentation, first measurement |
| 3 | 12 to 24 months | Integration, analytics, proactive management |
| 4 | 24 to 36 months | Optimization, innovation |

Shorten or drop phases to fit the user's horizon.

## Tracking

For each roadmap item: an owner role, a KPI that shows progress, an intermediate milestone with a measurable target, and a review cadence (monthly, quarterly or annually, set by how fast the domain changes).
