# Contribution and conceptual-foundation passes

## Contribution: venue and expectations

- Venue threshold: journal mission and aims, selectivity and acceptance standard, expected breadth and audience reach, article-type threshold, whether the venue rates significance explicitly or prioritizes rigor regardless of perceived impact.
- Expected contribution by article type: original research (new evidence, explanation or intervention); theory (new framework, formal result or explanatory account); methods (meaningful capability with validated advantage); dataset, resource or software (durable, documented, usable community value); systematic review (comprehensive, transparent, decision-relevant synthesis); replication or null result (meaningful test of an important claim or practice); clinical, policy or engineering work (credible pathway to consequential use); commentary or review (distinctive synthesis or agenda-setting value).
- Keep the dimensions apart: originality (what is new), significance (why it matters), validity (is it supported), relevance (for whom), durability (beyond a narrow moment, dataset or benchmark), fit (substantial enough for this venue).

## Normalize the contribution claim

- Collect the claims from the title, abstract, highlights, cover letter, introduction gap statement, aims, results, discussion and conclusion, and from any dataset, tool or reproducibility claim.
- Write one neutral sentence of the form: this manuscript contributes X by doing Y, relative to Z, with evidence E. Name the object, comparator, difference, rationale, evidence, scope and consequence.
- Classify claim strength: existence, superiority, priority, generality, causal, mechanistic, translation or deployment, field-changing.
- Internal defects: the contribution changes across sections; the abstract overstates the body; the discussion introduces a new contribution; a claim depends on unreported evidence; claimed novelty differs from the actual technical result; the practical implication is stronger than the evidence.

## Originality

- Novelty type: new question or framing; new theory; new method; new evidence; new dataset, benchmark, system or resource; new synthesis or application.
- Comparators: closest conceptual, method, empirical and dataset predecessors; strongest current baseline; the authors' most related prior work; concurrent preprints or publications; the standard of care or accepted benchmark. A comparator is adequate when it addresses the same question with a comparable population, metric and resource budget, is current, and is fairly implemented. Mismatches: straw-man or obsolete baseline, unequal compute, data or sample budget, different outcome, population, task or protocol, selective choice of comparators.
- Authors' prior work: legitimate extension (materially new question, analysis, population or validation, with transparent cross-reference); fragmentation; redundancy; duplicate publication (substantial overlap without clear visible reference).
- Relation to prior findings: discovery, replication, confirmation, contradiction or correction, incremental extension. Do not require discovery when replication or correction is itself valuable, and do not accept incremental framing as transformative without evidence.
- Calibration: words such as first, novel, unprecedented, state-of-the-art, general or breakthrough need support and qualifiers (first in which population, setting, task or metric; better under which constraints). Overclaiming patterns: terminology without substance; marginal benchmark gain presented as conceptual advance; new split presented as new task; larger sample alone presented as new finding; new parameterization presented as new method; single case presented as general solution; new application without showing why transfer matters.

## Significance

- Problem importance: scientific, clinical or public-health, technical, policy or societal, theoretical.
- Magnitude of advance against the baseline state: effect size, performance, precision, efficiency, scale, reliability or safety, generality, reproducibility, usability. Material only when it exceeds noise, any minimal important difference or operational threshold, justifies its added cost or complexity, persists under fair comparison, and is not driven by one subgroup, dataset, leakage or unequal resources.
- Consequence: theoretical change, method adoption potential, evidence value, pathway to practical use and translation readiness, durability beyond a narrow benchmark or trend.
- Impact claims: map each to its intermediate steps, the evidence for each, untested assumptions and risks. Overreach: societal claim from a narrow lab study; clinical claim from a nonclinical surrogate; policy prescription from a descriptive association; general engineering claim from one benchmark; causal implication from observational data; scalability claim without deployment evidence. Credible language: may enable (plausible, untested), demonstrates (direct evidence), improves under tested conditions (bounded), supports rather than proves, requires external validation.
- Calibration checks: novelty and significance are independent (new but trivial, important but confirmatory); a high significance claim needs high evidentiary support; a strong contribution claim cannot compensate for invalid methods; wider scope needs broader validation; a causal claim needs causal identification.

## Literature verification

- Targets: closest conceptual predecessors, methods and baselines, empirical studies, the authors' related work, central factual premises, central novelty and impact claims, contradictory or null evidence.
- Search boundary: topic, method, population, outcome and timeframe; peer-reviewed work versus preprints; databases suited to the discipline; search date; depth proportionate to the claimed novelty.
- Searches: exact contribution wording, title phrase and method name; conceptual decomposition (problem with population, intervention with outcome, method with task, benchmark with metric); author linkage; backward and forward citations of central predecessors; synonyms, historical and adjacent-discipline terms.
- Priority classes: prior art fully anticipates; partially anticipates; closest comparator; concurrent independent work; legitimate extension; potential redundant or duplicate publication. Overlap concerns go to the editor confidentially, with specific sources and locations and without asserting intent.
- Literature balance: seminal work, current competitors and reviews, recent corrections or retractions, contradictory evidence; citations are primary for central claims, represent the source accurately (population, magnitude, causation, date and preprint status), and self-citation does not distort the field.
- Reference requests: request a reference only when it materially improves accuracy, novelty assessment, theory, method choice or interpretation (relevance); name the location, the claim affected and whether it is required or optional (specificity); prefer the most authoritative source over long lists (minimality); drop any request that serves the reviewer, an associate or a journal (self-interest); do not make acceptance conditional on citing a work unless it corrects a material scholarly error (decision independence).

## Conceptual foundations

- Problem formulation: reconstruct the problem neutrally; test semantic, analytical and decision clarity; define population, system, context and inference scope; check the unit of analysis; define each construct and its operationalization; list explicit and implicit assumptions and boundary conditions.
- Background and theory: verify premise-critical background claims against their cited evidence; judge the rigor and balance of prior research; identify the framework, mechanism, logic model or epistemological stance and whether it explains rather than decorates; consider competing frameworks; keep fact, evidence-based inference, assumption and speculation apart.
- Questions and hypotheses: classify and rank objectives; check specificity and evaluability; check derivation of hypotheses and their link to measurement and analysis; compare with any registration, protocol or registered-report plan; check consistency across abstract, introduction, methods, results and discussion.
- Gap and contribution logic: the gap is genuine when the key, seminal, current and contradictory work was considered. Rhetorical gaps include few or no studies claimed without search, terminology difference presented as absence, the authors' method preference defined as the gap, and omission of a direct predecessor. Test that the contribution addresses the stated gap directly and sufficiently, and that the evidence fits the contribution type (causal claim needs identification; method superiority needs fair baseline, ablation, uncertainty and validation; generalization needs external validation; resource value needs documentation, access and quality).
- Audits: trace each central claim back through inference, result, analysis, measurement, design, question, theory, gap and premise and flag broken links; list central assumptions with their role, testability and mitigation; name alternative explanations (confounding, reverse causation, selection, measurement artifact, leakage, competing mechanism, chance or multiple testing) and whether design or analysis addresses them; flag population, setting, temporal, causal, mechanistic, generalizability and practical-impact inflation.

## Ratings

| Dimension | Scale |
|---|---|
| Originality | High: substantial, clearly differentiated new contribution. Moderate: meaningful extension, validation, correction or resource advance. Limited: modest extension with narrow novelty. Absent or unsubstantiated: central claim materially anticipated or inadequately differentiated. |
| Significance | High: substantial likely effect on field, method, practice or policy. Moderate: meaningful advance for a substantial specialist audience. Limited: narrow, incremental or weakly consequential. Unsubstantiated: claimed importance exceeds evidence or venue threshold. |
| Problem formulation | Strong: clear, bounded, consequential, coherent and evaluable. Adequate: limited definitional or scope gaps. Weak: important ambiguities impede evaluation. Defective: core problem incoherent, unanswerable or mismatched to the contribution. |
| Background and theory | Strong: accurate, balanced, theory-driven and connected to the study. Adequate: credible with limited omissions. Weak: partial or selective foundation or unclear theory use. Defective: premise, framework or logic does not support the question or contribution. |
| Research questions | Strong: specific, evaluable, grounded, prespecified where appropriate and aligned. Adequate: answerable with minor ambiguity. Weak: diffuse, partly post hoc or incompletely aligned. Defective: not evaluable, not answerable by the design or inconsistent across the manuscript. |
| Contribution logic | Strong: genuine material gap, direct contribution, evidence fit, calibrated scope. Adequate: credible with bounded overstatement. Weak: partial mismatch among gap, contribution, evidence or scope. Defective: rhetorical gap, unsupported contribution or claims outside the design. |

## Pass-specific cautions

Do not conflate unfamiliarity with novelty or novelty with importance; do not penalize robust replication or null and corrective work for lacking positive novelty; do not claim priority without traceable evidence; do not impose a theory where the research tradition does not require one; do not treat a missing formal hypothesis as poor scholarship in exploratory, qualitative, descriptive or resource work; do not demand post hoc preregistration; do not call a gap manufactured without specific literature-based support.
