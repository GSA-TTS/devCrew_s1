# Distractor engineering by difficulty

Every distractor stands for one named misconception, reasoning error or typical mistake. Random wrong statements are not distractors.

## Common rules

- Options per item: 3 or 4 in total (one key and 2 or 3 distractors). Three-option items measure about as well as four- or five-option items and are faster to write and answer.
- Homogeneous: the same content category and grammatical structure as the key.
- Mutually exclusive: no two options can both be correct.
- No clues: no option stands out by length, detail, wording or grammatical fit with the stem.

## Where misconceptions come from

Prefer real evidence over guesses, in this order:

1. Errors the user supplies: wrong answers from earlier constructed-response or open questions, student interview notes, error logs or graded work.
2. Misconceptions that are well documented for the topic.
3. Systematic variation of the correct answer (below). Mark a distractor built this way as a hypothesised misconception that the pilot must confirm.

## Systematic generation from the key

- Replace one key feature with a plausible alternative.
- Remove a critical element while keeping the surface structure.
- Reverse a cause-and-effect relationship.
- Apply a correct principle in the wrong context.

## By difficulty

Easy: confusions a student without the knowledge would make.

Medium: common implementation errors and partly correct understandings that show incomplete comprehension, for example:

- the right steps in the wrong sequence;
- a step's purpose mischaracterised (an essential element treated as optional);
- the concept conflated with a similar one.

Hard: sophisticated misconceptions that a knowledgeable student might hold:

- Partially correct solution: fixes one aspect of the problem while creating a secondary issue.
- Expert blind spot: a standard practice that is suboptimal in this specific context.
- Paradoxical option: counterintuitive yet valid only under assumptions the stem does not grant.
- Complexity trap: sounds more sophisticated than the key but contains a subtle logical flaw, or undermines the principle it claims to improve.

## Record for each distractor

- The option text.
- The misconception or error it represents, in one phrase.
- Its source: supplied student errors, documented misconception, or systematic variation (hypothesised).
