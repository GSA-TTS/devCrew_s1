# Condensing techniques, scaffolding and red flags

Use the technique names below in the Techniques applied column of the change log and the before-and-after samples.

## 1. Techniques by section type

Conceptual sections:
- Replace lengthy definitions with concise standard definitions.
- Use a worked example instead of an abstract explanation.
- Convert paragraphs to bulleted lists where appropriate.
- Remove historical background unless it is essential.

Procedural sections:
- Eliminate prerequisite information that is covered elsewhere.
- Use numbered steps without explanatory text between steps.
- Consolidate sub-steps where possible.
- Remove "how to" from headings; the procedure structure implies it.

Reference sections:
- Use tables instead of prose for specifications.
- Eliminate redundant examples.
- Reference external standards rather than duplicating them.
- Use consistent, minimal formatting.
- Minimalist removal of explanatory text does not suit comprehensive reference material; condense its wording and layout instead.

Practice material (problems, exercises):
- Replace several practice problems with fewer worked examples plus one practice problem (for example, five problems become two worked examples and one problem).
- Structure each worked example as problem statement, solution steps, a brief rationale for each step, and the key principles it shows.
- Remove lengthy explanatory text by embedding the principles in the examples.
- Vary surface features across examples, and use self-explanation prompts rather than complete explanations.

Other sections (introductions, overviews, transitions): apply only the sentence-level techniques of part 2, and keep the signposting of part 6.

## 2. Sentence-level techniques (every sentence)

Concise writing principles:
- Meaningless words: delete empty framing such as it is important to note that.
- Redundant words: delete pairs and doublings that repeat one meaning (first and foremost, basic fundamentals).
- Inferable content: delete what readers can infer from context or from their role.
- Complicated wording: replace wordy nominal phrases with the plain verb or noun (utilization of becomes using).
- Negatives to affirmatives: state the action directly (do not fail to verify becomes verify).

Additional techniques:
- Active voice.
- Cut filler words (basically, essentially, actually).
- Trim long sentences, aiming for 15 to 20 words per sentence.
- Replace multi-word phrases with single precise words.

## 3. Structure techniques

Minimalist principles:
- Remove explanatory text that users can infer from context.
- Focus on the essential task completion steps; convert lengthy descriptions to task-focused guidance.
- Eliminate redundant error-prevention scaffolding that creates dependency.
- Prefer just-in-time information over comprehensive background.
- Limits: minimalism can be too basic for advanced practitioners and needs care for mixed-expertise audiences.

Information mapping:
- Chunking: 5 plus or minus 1 items per chunk online, 7 plus or minus 2 on paper; break large paragraphs into scannable lists.
- Labeling: give every information block a descriptive label.
- Consistency: one term, style and format per thing throughout.
- Relevance: keep what the reader's purpose needs; separate need to know from nice to know, and move supplementary content to an appendix.
- Consolidate redundant blocks across sections.

Cognitive load:
- Reduce extraneous load: remove decorative graphics that do not support learning, redundant text-image repetition and split attention (integrate text with its diagram); simplify navigation and layout; keep formatting and terminology consistent.
- Optimize germane load: replace lengthy explanations with worked examples; use progressive disclosure for complex procedures; fade scaffolding gradually; add self-explanation prompts at key points.
- Manage intrinsic load: sequence from simple to complex; present prerequisite knowledge and conceptual models before detailed procedures.

Semantic density:
- Use precise technical terminology, and the established vocabulary of the field's framework consistently.
- Condense related concepts into single statements.
- Reference an established standard rather than re-explaining it.

## 4. Meaning units

- A meaning unit is the smallest piece that carries one meaning (a paragraph or smaller). Code each by function: concept, definition, prerequisite, procedure step, worked example, practice problem, background.
- Classify each unit essential, important or supplementary by its criticality to the content's objectives.
- Compliance-critical units (regulatory or legal requirements, required disclaimers and warnings) cannot be reduced.
- Redundant units express the same meaning in more than one place; keep one complete instance.

## 5. Scaffolding for the audience

- Keep scaffolding at the level the target audience needs; fade excessive scaffolding that creates dependency; remove scaffolding below the audience's expertise.
- Over-scaffolding, candidates for removal: detailed explanations of basics the audience knows; excessive error prevention; redundant examples that add no new perspective; step-by-step guidance for tasks the audience performs independently.
- Under-scaffolding, must preserve: preparation before abrupt moves to complex material; prerequisite explanations; worked examples for complex procedures; self-check opportunities.

## 6. Comprehension first

- Never sacrifice clarity for brevity: if the shorter version is ambiguous, use the longer clear version.
- Preserve examples that aid understanding: one clear example beats a lengthy explanation, varied examples beat repeated similar ones, and examples showing common errors are valuable.
- Maintain adequate context: just enough for correct interpretation, prerequisite explanations not covered elsewhere, and orientation information for navigation.
- Keep signposting even when it adds words: headings and subheadings, transitions between major topics, summary statements for complex sections, preview statements.

Red flags (stop and re-evaluate the section):
- Removing content makes the remaining content confusing.
- A subject matter expert could not quickly understand the condensed version.
- More than one interpretation is possible.
- The condensed version needs an external reference to be understood.
- All examples were removed from a conceptual section.
- Procedural steps became unclear or ambiguous.

Resolutions:
- Add back minimal context.
- Include one clarifying example.
- Rewrite for clarity before condensing further.
- Accept a slightly lower compression ratio for that section.
