# Lean policy template and drafting rules

## The ten sections, in this order

1. Title and version information: policy name; version number or date; classification (board-approved governance policy or operational policy); owning role or committee; next review date.
2. Purpose: a short statement of why the policy exists, which risk or obligation it addresses, and how it supports the mission.
3. Scope and applicability: who it applies to (employees, volunteers, contractors, board members), and what (programs, data types, geographies); when it applies (its trigger, such as any use of a generative AI tool with donor data, or any staff involvement in procurement); what is out of scope or covered by another document.
4. Definitions (where needed): plain-language definitions of key terms only; point to a central glossary where the organization has one. If no term needs defining, write "None needed."
5. Principles and commitments: high-level statements of the standards or outcomes the organization commits to (for example fairness, transparency, non-discrimination, donor privacy, safeguarding).
6. Roles and responsibilities: who is accountable, who decides and who supports (board committees, executive leadership, managers, staff, volunteers, vendors).
7. Policy requirements: concise, numbered statements of what must or must not be done, using "must", "must not" and, where a requirement is advisory, "should"; each written so that compliance can be tested and audited.
8. Procedural references: the separate procedures, playbooks or checklists that put the policy into practice and can change more often without board re-approval. Name them; where one does not yet exist, list it as to be written.
9. Compliance, exceptions and escalation: how compliance is monitored; how an exception is requested and who approves it; how breaches or concerns are reported, including the whistleblowing channel.
10. Review and change process: review frequency and the triggers for an earlier review, who leads the review, and how updates are approved and communicated.

## Drafting rules

- Short sentences, active voice, "must" and "must not" for binding rules.
- Group related requirements under clear sub-headings, not long narrative paragraphs.
- Draft from real scenarios and edge cases of how people actually work, including remote work and digital tools.
- Keep the policy outcome-oriented: state what must be achieved. Move step-by-step detail into the procedures listed in section 8.
- Avoid duplication: where an umbrella policy (data protection, information security, AI governance) already holds a rule, cross-reference it instead of repeating it.
- Keep each requirement only if it passes this test: it materially reduces a risk, meets a legal or funder obligation, or directly supports the mission. Otherwise remove it.
- Use the least constraint that manages the risk and meets the obligation; add no approval or documentation step the risk does not need.

## Skeleton

```text
# <Policy name>
Version: <n or date> | Classification: <board-approved governance | operational>
Owner: <role or committee> | Approved by: <body> on <date> | Next review: <date>

## 1. Purpose
## 2. Scope and applicability
## 3. Definitions
## 4. Principles and commitments
## 5. Roles and responsibilities
## 6. Policy requirements
6.1 <Sub-heading>
  6.1.1 <Who> must <do what>.
## 7. Procedural references
## 8. Compliance, exceptions and escalation
## 9. Review and change process
```

The skeleton numbers the body sections from 1 because the title block is unnumbered; keep that order.
